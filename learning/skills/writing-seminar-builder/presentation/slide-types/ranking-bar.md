# Slide type: Ranking (bar)

`purpose:` Prove · `when-to-use (trigger):` you need to compare a handful of discrete items by one magnitude and the point is *which is biggest* (or where the line is drawn), so a sorted bar lets the eye rank them at a glance · `density:` V · `element(s):` [ranking-bar](../elements/ranking-bar.md) · `animation:` 2 builds (bars cascade top to bottom, then the focal bar lights gold with the takeaway) · `lineage:` new in the Prove family; the sorted-bar default from the financial-data-decks learnings (Zelazny "rank, which is biggest" maps to a sorted bar), built bespoke in CSS like the other bar charts, sharing the one-focal-series-mute-the-rest discipline of every data slide · `build-priority:` consulting / data set (after the first wave), alongside trend, waterfall, and the 2x2 matrix.

Reach for this type when the beat is a comparison of named, discrete items on a single measure and the audience question is "which one leads" or "where does the cutoff fall": departments by hours saved, use cases by adoption, vendors by error rate, processes by cycle time. Sort the bars descending so the ranking is the message, mute every bar to quiet navy, and let one gold bar carry the point the slide is making (the leader, the one you are recommending, the one above the line). The value labels sit at the bar ends so the eye reads order first and exact magnitude second. Do not reach for it when the items are time periods in sequence (that is a trend column), when the parts sum to a whole (composition), or when you are explaining a delta between two totals (waterfall). Keep the field to roughly four to seven bars; more than that and the slide becomes a table the audience has to mine rather than a ranking they can read. Every figure carries a named, cited source, and per the F3 client-facing rule the magnitudes are illustrative or benchmark units, never prices.

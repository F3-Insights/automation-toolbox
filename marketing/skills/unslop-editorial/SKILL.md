# Unslop editorial

## Who is reading

The audience the owner describes (setting `audience`), for example finance leaders at mid-sized companies. By default, a business decision maker: a COO, a CFO, a division head, an operations lead. They chose to read this or they are sitting in a room where the owner is speaking. Nobody is paying for this particular page, so attention is the only currency, and it runs out fast.

They are not AI enthusiasts. They have heard vendor pitches and discounted them. What they want is a way to think about a decision they already know they have to make. They came for the business outcome, and AI is how that outcome gets reached. They will forgive a rough edge. They will not forgive being sold to, being graded, or being walked through a paragraph that turns out to say nothing.

## How the universal principles bite here

Principle 5 is relaxed. This is the one register where voice is expected. A seminar script with no person in it is worse than one carrying an opinion the audience disagrees with. First person, a stated position, an admission that something did not work, a rhythm that varies because a real person wrote it, all fine here and often required. Principle 5 survives in one narrow form. Voice means the writer's actual personality showing through rather than a performance of personality, which is the subject of the first principle below.

Principle 1 is the one that voice most often gets used to dodge. A sentence in a LinkedIn post has to tell the reader something even if it also sounds good. If it only sounds good, it is decoration.

Principle 2 still binds. Editorial writing is where coined labels breed, because they feel like insight. A named framework the audience has to learn before they can follow the point costs more than it gives back.

Principle 3 binds hardest here. Marketing copy that could run under any consultancy's logo is the default failure of the whole category.

Principle 4 is the reason this register exists. The reader's problem is the subject and AI is the method.

## Principles specific to this reader

### Voice should come from having a position

Goal: the human quality in a sentence comes from the writer thinking something, not from the sentence being shaped to feel like thought. AI writing imitates the shape. It builds escalating series, matched halves, and closing fragments, because those are what conviction sounds like from the outside. A reader who has read a thousand of them recognizes the shape and stops trusting the writer.

Diagnostic question: if I rewrote this in flat, ordinary word order, would anything be lost except the cadence? If nothing is lost, the cadence was doing the work, and the work was fake.

Example: "What the audit found, why the backlog keeps growing, and what only this room can decide." Three items that lengthen in order, which is a rhythm and not a thought. Say what the meeting covers and let the list be whatever length it actually is.

### A memorable line has to be true of this one situation

Goal: the lines worth keeping are the ones that could only be said about this company, this project, this week. Maxims fail because they generalize. A sentence that sounds wise about consulting in general tells the reader nothing about the thing in front of them, and it reads as filler dressed up.

Diagnostic question: could this line appear, unchanged, in a post about a different client or a different industry? If yes, cut it or replace it with the specific fact it was standing in for.

Example: "Option 1 is a project; Option 2 is a habit." Nothing in it is about the actual options. Name what each option costs and how long it takes.

### Show the concrete case before the lesson

Goal: business audiences believe examples and discount claims. The owner teaches demo first and explanation second, and written editorial should follow the same order. An abstract claim followed by an example asks the reader to hold an unsupported idea in mind. The example first gives them something to hold.

Diagnostic question: by the end of the second sentence, has the reader been handed something they could picture, a number, a screen, a specific decision someone made? If the opening is still operating at the level of categories, move the example up.

Example: instead of opening a newsletter with a claim about measurement problems, open with the hours-saved number a client reported and what it turned out to include.

### Add to what the reader already does

Goal: the audience is competent and running a business that works. Every line should read as a trusted advisor adding something to their toolkit. Framing that implies they have been doing it wrong loses the room, even when the observation is correct, and it is the fastest way to make the smartest person in the second row stop listening.

Diagnostic question: does this sentence say the reader has a gap, or does it say a new factor exists that is worth planning for?

Example: "Most ROI models miss this cost" becomes "AI introduces this cost category for the first time."

## Keep

Do not strip these while editing. They are the register.

Opinions. If the owner thinks a tool is overhyped or an approach is wrong, that belongs on the page. Neutral survey writing is the sound of nobody.

First person. "I" is correct here. So is naming what they have actually built, what they pay per month, and what they have not tried.

Real enthusiasm. "I am glad to be back in a warehouse for this one" is fine. It is a fact about the writer, plainly stated.

Specific detail, including detail that complicates the point. Names, numbers, dates, the tangent that came up in a session. Mess is evidence of a person.

Honest uncertainty. "I do not actually use this much, to be honest" builds more trust with this audience than confidence would.

Varied sentence length, when the ideas are genuinely different sizes.

## Mechanics

No em dashes and no en dashes anywhere, editorial writing included. Use a period or a comma, and do not substitute parentheses.

Sentence case for every heading and slide title.

No emoji.

Straight quotes.

No prices in anything public facing. Name the service and let pricing be a follow up conversation.

Every number carries a source the reader could check.

Vendor agnostic. Name a specific tool when that tool is the point, but the piece is not selling one.

## Self-audit

1. Read the piece aloud. Where does it sound like a speech instead of a person talking? Those are the sentences that were built for rhythm.
2. Pick the three best sentences. Could any of them run in a competitor's newsletter without editing? If so they are not the best sentences, they are the most familiar ones.
3. Where is the first concrete thing the reader can picture, and how far down the page is it?
4. Does any sentence imply the reader has been doing something wrong?
5. Is there a position in here that a reasonable person could disagree with? If not, nobody wrote this.

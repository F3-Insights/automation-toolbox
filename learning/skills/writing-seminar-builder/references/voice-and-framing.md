# Voice and Framing

The rules governing how seminar content speaks to the audience. Complements `slide-formatting.md` (per-slide format) and `narrative-arc.md` (story structure). This file is about the *posture*: how it should *feel* to the executive in row 2.

Read this in Step 1 (during interrogation) and apply it through Step 4 (deck draft) and Step 5 (iterate).

---

## The core posture: trusted advisor, not consultant grading homework

The audience came in good faith. They have working capex math. They've made good decisions in their careers. The seminar's job is to be the *trusted advisor adding to their toolkit*, not the consultant who showed up to grade their work.

This shapes every choice in the deck: titles, slide text, speaker scripts, even the punchy send-off. If a line in the deck would make a smart, senior member of the audience in row 2 (for a finance audience, the CFO) feel lectured at, soften it.

---

## Rule 1: Positive and additive, never adversarial

**Never imply the audience is doing something wrong.** Frame considerations as *"unique to AI"* or *"additive to good math"*, never *"your math is missing X"* or *"you're overshooting."*

### Substitution table

| Adversarial (avoid) | Positive / additive (use) |
|---|---|
| "Your math is missing X" | "X is unique to AI / worth planning for" |
| "Your math doesn't have a line item for X" | "Add a line item for X; it's AI-specific" |
| "Your model is wrong" | "Update your model with these new considerations" |
| "You're going to overshoot" | "Plan a buffer for the AI-specific overhead" |
| "Most ROI models don't capture this" | "AI introduces this cost category for the first time" |
| "Companies miss / discover / skip this" | "Companies that plan for this land their numbers" |
| "What you're getting wrong" | "What's worth fine-tuning for AI" |
| "Your claims will collapse" | "Make your claims board-survivable" (positive outcome) |
| "Most companies fail at this" | "Here's what good looks like" |
| "Your existing math is insufficient" | "Your existing math is necessary; these considerations make it sufficient" |
| "You're systematically under-valuing X" | "The math benefits from explicitly including X" |

### When tough framing IS appropriate

- Quoting third-party data ("Gartner found..." / "RAND data shows..."): the attribution distances the claim from the audience
- Naming general industry patterns without pointing at the audience ("Most companies discover..."). Careful here: "companies that plan for X" is usually better
- The contrarian opener that names a *common industry belief* (like the hours-saved fallacy) is fine because it punches up at the industry's habit, not at the audience's competence

---

## Rule 2: Forward-looking, not retroactive

Speak to *what to consider next*, not *what was done wrong before*. The default audience is **decision-makers planning future investment**: AI-curious, looking to weigh pros and cons. Not people defending past decisions (a narrower case).

Sales-friendly forward-looking energy is the right register: *"as you make these decisions, here's what to consider"*, not *"here's where your existing decisions went wrong."*

---

## Rule 3: Acknowledge audience expertise

When introducing substantive content, acknowledge what they already know before pivoting to the new material. Example:

> *"You already do this for every capex decision: NPV, payback,
> opportunity cost. These three lenses have served you well. They're
> not wrong for AI. They're just not sufficient on their own."*

This builds rapport. It says: *I respect what you bring; let me add to it.* It does NOT condescend or imply they've been doing it wrong.

---

## Rule 4: Use "the math" or "we", not "your math"

When describing common pitfalls, frame around the *topic*, not the *audience*. Distance the criticism from the listener.

| Avoid | Use |
|---|---|
| "Your ROI model is missing..." | "AI ROI math has a new category for..." |
| "Your team won't catch this" | "This is the kind of thing that gets caught upstream when..." |
| "You haven't budgeted for X" | "The fine-tune: budgets work best with an explicit line for X" |

---

## Rule 5: Empowering send-offs

The punchy send-off slide (the final content slide before the offer) should *empower* the audience, not correct them. State the positive outcome they get from applying the lesson.

**Good:**
- *"You don't need better AI claims. You need claims that survive the next board review."*
- *"Better framing, not better tools, is what separates AI as advisor from AI as expensive yes-man."*
- *"The shift: from reactive analysis to forward-looking decision support."*

**Bad:**
- *"Stop making bad ROI claims."* (corrective)
- *"Most companies are doing this wrong."* (accusatory)
- *"Don't let your AI investment fail."* (fear-based)

---

## Rule 6: Sell the positive outcome, even when teaching about risk

When the topic naturally skews negative (risk, compliance, governance, security, failure modes), frame the seminar around the **positive outcome that good practice enables**, not around the disasters to avoid.

| ✗ Negative-led (avoid) | ✓ Positive-led (use) |
|---|---|
| "Here's how to avoid AI disasters" | "Here's how smart governance unlocks AI at scale" |
| "Don't be the next Samsung" | "Companies running AI at scale with confidence, and the governance patterns that make it possible" |
| "5 ways AI can hurt your business" | "5 patterns for unlocking AI safely: insights from companies doing it well" |
| "AI governance: what your CIO is missing" | "Smart governance: how to unlock AI across your workforce" |

**Insights & learnings from companies doing it well > cautionary tales.** Negative case studies (Samsung leaks, failed AI layoffs, vendor lock-in horror stories) are still useful; they appear as *evidence inside the positive frame*, never as the lead or central theme.

Default content split for risk-flavored topics:
- ~60–70% positive case studies + patterns + what good looks like
- ~20–30% specific cautionary examples (used as supporting evidence)
- ~10% direct enablement: the actionable governance framework

**Why this matters for a firm that sells enablement:** the offer is AI *enablement*, not risk prevention. The seminar's job is to make the audience want to deploy AI more confidently, not to scare them away from it. Risk topics still get serious treatment, but the framing is *"here's the unlock; here's how to do it right"*, not *"here's the danger; here's how to avoid it."*

The rule holds for any business seminar selling enablement (training, consulting, transformation): lead with the positive outcome of the work, not the consequences of skipping it.

---

## Rule 7: Anti-patterns specific to vendor / hype voice

Reject phrasings that sound like vendor marketing or sales-bot boilerplate:

- *"Unlock the power of AI"*
- *"Transform your business"*
- *"AI-driven [anything]"*
- *"Revolutionary"*
- *"Game-changer"*
- *"Let AI do the work your team can't"* (a vendor-style line that reads as sales-y; reject anything in this register)

These phrases mark content as un-trustworthy to operators. They're fine for ads (debatably); they're poison for an executive seminar.

---

## The shipping test

Before locking each slide and script, ask:

1. **Would the smartest, most senior person in row 2 (for a finance audience, the CFO) feel I was lecturing them?** If yes, soften.
2. **Does this feel like a trusted advisor adding to their toolkit, or like a consultant grading their homework?** Advisor-tone wins.
3. **Is the framing forward-looking, or retroactive?** Forward wins unless the topic specifically requires looking back.
4. **Did I use "your math is missing" or any phrasing from the adversarial column?** If yes, substitute from the positive column.
5. **Does the punchy send-off empower, or correct?** Empower.
6. **For risk/governance topics: is the seminar selling the positive unlock, or just warning about disasters?** Sell the unlock; use cautionary tales as supporting evidence only.

If a slide fails any of the six, fix it before declaring the deck done.

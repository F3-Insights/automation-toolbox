# Unslop: client deliverables

Apply the five universal principles from the router first, then everything below. The shared symptom lists and pattern catalog live in the router appendix, not here.

## Who is reading

A client executive or steering group that is paying a lot of money for this work and is reading the document as the record of it. They skim headlines first, stop where a number or a name appears, and carry three or four points into a decision meeting where we are not in the room. They are not an audience for writing. They are deciding whether to fund, build, staff, or stop something, and the document is evidence.

## How the universal principles bite here

1. Information, not rhythm. A slide has few lines and each one is expensive. A clause that only balances the sentence has taken a spot a fact needed.
2. State the thing; do not name it. This is the hardest one for this reader. Coined names for findings ("the backlog", "the shrink lens", "the slotting engine") read as cute rather than as work product, and the client has to decode them before they can argue with them. Give the finding its literal name.
3. Specific to this reader, or gone. General sayings that would be true at any firm take up space the client is paying for. If it survives swapping the client name, delete it.
4. Write from the reader's side of the page. The client bought conclusions and decisions, not a narration of our process. What we read, re-read, or synthesized is not a finding.
5. Register is a promise. Matter of fact and professional throughout. Any line that reads as performance spends trust the engagement needs later.

## Principles specific to this reader

### Headlines carry the finding, not a hook

Goal: a reader who reads only the slide headlines should come away with the actual findings. The headline is the deliverable's index and its summary at the same time, so it has to be flat and complete rather than intriguing.

Diagnostic: if this headline were the only line on the slide, would the client know what we found, or only that we found something?

Before: "Counts are trustworthy. Receiving is not." After: "Cycle counts are accurate, but receipts are not posted the day they arrive."

The rejected version splits one finding into two short beats for effect and swaps "accurate" for "trustworthy", which implies something about people that the evidence did not say. Same pattern in "Reordering runs on gut feel, and freight runs on panic", which became "Reorder points are set by hand and expedited freight is reactive."

### A recommendation names the work to be done

Goal: every recommendation title should be assignable. Someone should be able to read it and know what would get built, tracked, or changed, without reading the body.

Diagnostic: could a project manager put this title on a task and hand it to a person?

Before: "Revive the shelved slotting engine." After: "Build the bin-slotting report recommended in the last review."

The same edit applied across the set: "Add the shrink lens." became "Track shrink by warehouse zone." "Put cycle counting back in its lane." became "Use cycle counts to correct stock records only." "Grab the low-hanging fruit" became "Deliver the three small fixes."

### Our process is not content unless it changes a decision

Goal: the activity list and the body text should describe what the client now knows, not what we did to know it. Effort is not evidence, and listing it invites the client to price it.

Diagnostic: does this line change what the client knows or must decide? If it only shows that we worked, cut it.

Before, in a list of what we had done since kickoff: "Every receiving log since January, reviewed line by line." After: cut entirely. Effort is not the second thing to say about the last few weeks, and it probably does not belong on the list at all.

### No line exists to close, transition, or reassure

Goal: a deliverable ends sections and slides on facts. Summary lines, maxims, and framing sentences carry no information and read as the loudest AI tell in the document.

Diagnostic: delete the sentence. Does any fact, number, name, or decision disappear? If not, it was there for shape.

Before: "The answer decides the fix." After: "Which cause it is decides whether we change the process or the system."

Deleted outright in the same pass, with nothing put in their place: "The team's goodwill is a real asset." "This is a true choice, not a rubber stamp. Option 1 is a project; Option 2 is a habit." "Wherever the counts lead, we go." Also cut the subtitle "What the counts showed, why it keeps happening, and what only this room can decide", which read as contrived, and the tagline "Inventory accuracy. Process first, system second", which was replaced with a plain statement of what the deck covers.

## Keep

- Paraphrased interview quotes stay conversational. "I recount that aisle every Monday." "Nobody updates the bin after a putaway." Polishing these makes them read as written rather than heard, which destroys their value as evidence.
- Numbered chips, year chips, and category eyebrows are fine when they are literal: "Finding 1 · Receiving", "FY2025", "Recommendation 3".
- Numbers, dollar figures, dates, system names, role titles, and metric definitions. These are the reason the document exists. Compress around them, never through them.
- Direct statements of disagreement or risk. Flat delivery of an unwelcome finding is not something to soften.

## Mechanics

- No em dashes or en dashes anywhere. Write ranges as "30 to 45" or "Weeks 2-3". If a thought needs separation, end the sentence or use a comma.
- Sentence case for headings, except proper nouns. Heading names are literal: "Conclusions" rather than "Where we land", "Activity since kickoff" rather than "How we got here", "Decision Required" rather than "The Decision This Group Owns".
- Every bullet carries a concrete fact, number, name, or decision. A bullet that only frames or transitions comes out.
- When a readout is requested, hold bullets to about 16 words with no filler.
- No colon-label bullets. The label restates the line and then makes the reader parse two fragments. Before: "Dock scanning: receipts post when the pallet lands, with handhelds doing the entry." After: "Receipts post when the pallet is scanned at the dock, instead of from the paper packing slip at the end of the day."
- Coined labels inside bullets get replaced by the plain sequence of events. Before: "The shelving. The slotting engine was put on hold..." After: "The slotting report was postponed until the warehouse system upgrade. The upgrade went live in March. The report was never built."
- Prefer concrete subjects over abstract ones. "Incentives anchored on lines picked now select for the wrong behavior" became "Paying on lines picked rewards speed over accuracy."
- No colloquialisms. "Neither moved the needle" became "Neither changed the error rate."
- Slide subtitles are optional. If the subtitle only restates or decorates the title, drop it rather than rewriting it.

This rewrite (an invented example) shows the level to aim at. "The approval design. One word, 'approved,' meant three sign-offs with three limits. The fix: owner signs, AP matches, controller backstops..." becomes "Stop using the word 'approved' on its own, because it meant three different sign-offs with three different dollar limits. Instead: the budget owner approves the spend, AP confirms the purchase order and receipt match, and the controller signs only invoices over the policy limit. Also, retire the paper routing slip." It is longer and it is better, because every added word is a specific instruction.

## Self-audit

Ask these before returning the edit.

1. Reading only the headlines in order, does the client have the findings and the decisions?
2. Is there any coined name, nickname, or clever heading left that the client would have to decode?
3. Which sentences could be deleted without losing a fact, number, name, or decision? Delete them now.
4. Does any line describe what we did rather than what the client learns or must decide?
5. Would a client paying a lot of money read any line here as performance rather than as work?

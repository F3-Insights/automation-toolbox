# Meridian Strategic History: Decisions We've Made and How They Aged

*Last revised: 2026-04. Owner: CEO.*

A running record of major decisions, what was decided, and how the decision aged. Pre-mortems should reference relevant prior decisions when surfacing kill-shot risks. This file is appended to but not edited: old entries stay even when the lessons get folded into our taxonomy.

---

## 2019: Considered acquiring engineering firm in Madison, WI (killed)

Founder met with their CEO over six months. They were a 12-engineer firm with strong dairy-processing chops we did not have in-house.

**Decision:** Killed. We chose to hire selectively instead.

**Outcome:** Validated. The firm was acquired by a larger systems integrator 14 months later. By 2022, three of their senior engineers had left. Our hiring path put 4 dairy-domain engineers on staff over 2020–2022 at lower cost and with cultural fit.

**Lesson:** When the gap is engineering depth in a new domain, hiring directly beats acquiring an engineering firm if our culture can absorb the hires.

---

## 2021: Added second product line in beverage end-market (committed)

Strategic question: extend from craft beer (our original beverage product) to specialty non-alcoholic and functional beverages.

**Decision:** Committed. ~$3M in tooling and engineering investment.

**Outcome:** Successful. The non-alc / functional segment is now ~30% of beverage revenue. Acquired Customer B (now 7% of total revenue) through this expansion.

**Lesson:** End-market extensions inside an existing skillset work when the engineering DNA is the same. Don't confuse "new product line" with "new business."

---

## 2022: Brought automation engineering in-house (committed)

We had been subcontracting automation/controls work to two systems integrators. Decision was to hire 6 senior automation engineers and build the capability internally.

**Decision:** Committed. ~$2M in salary + signing + equipment over 18 months.

**Outcome:** Successful. ROI achieved in 14 months on a fully-loaded basis. Customer perception improved: installs went from "Meridian + their automation partner" to "Meridian." Repeat rate on installs ticked up ~6 points.

**Lesson:** Building capability internally beats buying from systems integrators when the volume justifies. Watch for the moment the math flips. We did this 18 months too late.

## 2023: Considered acquiring competitor BrewSystems (killed at gate 2)

BrewSystems was a $40M competitor in our beverage end-market. They approached us. The pre-mortem identified culture integration as the kill-shot risk: their org was sales-led, ours is engineering-led, and they did not commission their own installs. We killed at gate 2.

**Decision:** Killed.

**Outcome:** Strongly validated. They were acquired by PE 6 months later. Within 18 months their operating margin had dropped from 11% to ~4%. Two of their three top engineers left.

**Lesson:** Culture mismatch is the kill-shot risk in 80% of M&A. The financial accretion model usually says yes; the culture model says no. Trust the culture model.

This is one of the cleanest examples in the firm's history of the pre-mortem framing catching a risk the financial analysis was hiding.

## 2024: Expanded sales team by 8 (committed, mixed outcome)

Pipeline growth required either more sales coverage or longer cycles. We chose to hire 8 sales reps over 2024.

**Decision:** Committed. ~$1.4M annualized incremental cost.

**Outcome:** Mixed. 5 of 8 missed quota in their first full year. 3 left the company. Net pipeline coverage improved, but ROI on the cohort was negative for 2024.

**Lesson:** We hired against a generic profile ("good B2B reps with industrial experience") and not against specific account-development plans. The 3 reps who hit quota were the ones we had named target accounts for at signing. Next time, hire against accounts, not against headcount targets.

This lesson is directly relevant to any plan that proposes "hire X to address Y" without naming specific accounts/markets/projects each hire is paired to.

## 2025: Considered second facility in Greenville, SC (killed)

The first version of the "second facility" question. We were 9 months earlier in our SE customer growth trajectory.

**Decision:** Killed. Three kill-shot risks named in the brief:
1. SE customer base wasn't anchored: top-3 SE customers were each <2 years into the relationship.
2. Engineering bench was thinner in 2025 than in 2026 (we've added 5 engineers since).
3. Greenville labor market analysis was thin.

**Outcome:** Killed proved right. One of the 3 SE customers shrank by 40% in 2025 due to a co-pack restructure on their end. The other two have stabilized. The Greenville-killed decision means we don't have $7M sunk into the wrong SE city.

**Lesson:** Second-facility decisions depend more on customer-base maturity in the target region than on near-term capacity needs at the existing facility. If the customers haven't anchored, the facility hasn't earned the right to exist.

**Direct relevance to current TX question:** SW customers (TX/AZ/NM) ARE anchored: average 4.5 years into the relationship, top-3 SW customers all on multi-year renewals.

## 2025: Killed offer to do co-development with PE-backed customer (killed)

A Customer offered us 18 months of co-developed product work in exchange for engineering equity and a discounted price. Killed within 3 weeks.

**Decision:** Killed.

**Outcome:** N/A: clean kill, no later regret.

**Lesson:** Our hard line on not taking engineering equity remains right. Anyone offering equity for engineering hours is funding their R&D with our team's time at below-market rates.

# Narrative Arc

Where the story bends, where the answer lands, and how long each part should run. This file is the structural counterpart to `slide-formatting.md` (which covers per-slide format) and `workflow-depth.md` (which covers required substantive content). Together they shape a seminar that holds attention without padding.

---

## The story shape (five beats)

Every seminar follows the same five-beat arc, in order:

| Beat | What it does | Share of total time | Typical slide count |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Hook** | Names the pain the audience already feels. Earns the next four minutes. | ~10% | 1 slide (Slide 1) |
| **2. Setup** | Assumptions slide + a single "what you're getting today and why" slide. | ~15% | 2 slides (Slides 2–3) |
| **3. The answer** | The reframe, the technique, the proof that it works. Lands by ~25% of the talk. | ~20% | 2–3 slides |
| **4. Expansion** | The bulk of the seminar. What does the answer look like operationalized? Includes the Workflow Backbone Diagram. | ~45% | 5–6 slides |
| **5. Send-off** | One punchy slide that crystallizes the central insight, then 1–2 offer slides. | ~10% | 2–3 slides |

The most common mistake: **burying the answer**. If you're three slides in and still describing the problem, the audience hasn't been given a reason to stay engaged. The answer slide is where the talk earns the next 20 minutes.

## Pacing math

Default pacing: **~2–3 minutes per content slide**, with title/assumptions/CTA at ~1 minute each.

| Duration | Total slides | Pacing |
|---|---|---|
| 25 min | 10–12 slides | ~2 min per content slide |
| 30 min | 12–13 slides | ~2.5 min per content slide |
| 45 min | 14–16 slides | only when the topic genuinely earns the air |

**Default is 25–30 min, not 45.** Executive audiences are time-poor; a tight 30-minute seminar that lands is worth more than a 45-minute one that drifts. Reach for 45 only when the topic genuinely cannot be told in 30.

Slide-count discipline:

- If a slide takes less than 90 seconds to talk through, it's probably a bullet that belongs on the previous slide.
- If a slide takes more than 4 minutes to talk through, it's probably two slides.
- The Workflow Backbone Diagram is the exception: it can earn 4 minutes alone because it's the centerpiece.

## Where the answer lands

**By minute 5–8 in a 30-minute seminar.** That's slide 3 or slide 4 of a 12-slide deck.

Practical rule: by the time you've used 25% of the time budget, the audience knows:

1. What the painful thing is (Hook + Setup)
2. Why it's happening (compressed into the Setup slide or the start of the Answer slide)
3. The technique or reframe that fixes it (the Answer slide)

Everything from minute 8 onward is *expansion*: what the answer looks like, how it operationalizes, when to use which variant, what good looks like. This is where the depth lives. The audience already knows where the seminar is going; now you're earning their trust by going deeper than they expected.

Anti-pattern: a four-slide problem-setup that never delivers the answer until slide 6 or 7. By then, the most engaged executives have started reading email.

## The punchy send-off slide

The last content slide, the one right before the offer cards, should be the slide an attendee remembers a week later.

**What it does:**

- Crystallizes the central insight in one visual or phrase
- Restates the shift from "the old way" to "the new way" in plain language
- Earns the offer that follows by making the gap between *knowing this* and *operationalizing it* obvious

**What it is NOT:**

- A summary slide listing every section
- A generic "what good looks like / what fails" slide (those belong earlier in the expansion)
- A "thank you for listening" slide
- A laundry list of takeaways

**Patterns that work:**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **Before / After comparison** | Left: "AI used as an audience" → Right: "AI used as an advisor." Two columns, minimal text, one image or icon per side. |
| **The shift** | A single sentence stated as a principle: *"Better framing, not better tools, is what separates AI as advisor from AI as expensive yes-man."* Big text, centered. |
| **One year from now** | What does the audience's strategic-decision practice look like 12 months after they apply this? Visual contrast. |
| **The monday-morning move** | A single concrete action the executive can take tomorrow that proves the principle in their own work. |

If the last content slide is *"What good looks like (and what fails)"* or anything that just lists bullets, it's the wrong slide. The send-off has rhythm.

## When to compress

Two slides should become one when they:

- Serve the same arc beat (e.g., "the problem" and "why the problem happens" are both Setup; collapse them)
- Cover the same operational question with adjacent angles (e.g., context and HITL can share a slide as "where context lives + where humans decide")
- Have so little content per slide that one slide could carry both without crowding

Don't compress when:

- The slides are at different beats of the arc (Answer + Expansion should stay separate)
- One of them is the Workflow Backbone Diagram (always its own slide)
- Compressing would force a slide above the ~40-word body-text budget from `visual-design.md`

## How this interacts with `workflow-depth.md`

`workflow-depth.md` requires a Workflow Backbone Diagram slide and coverage of the five recurring operational questions. With the 10–13 slide target:

- The backbone diagram is its own slide (centerpiece, non-negotiable)
- The five questions get covered across 3–4 expansion slides, with some questions combined onto the same slide
- A typical expansion arc: Backbone → Context+HITL → Sub-agents+Artifacts → Recursive improvement → Three patterns

That's 4 expansion slides covering 5 questions plus the backbone: 5 slides total in the expansion beat, which fits the 45% time share.

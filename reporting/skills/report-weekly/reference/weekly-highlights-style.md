# Weekly Highlights house style

How the weekly report is written, once the outline has said what it is about. The role profile (`weekly-report-profile.md` beside this file) decides the categories and their order; this file decides the sentences. `weekly-highlights-prompt.md` beside it condenses these rules into one pasteable prompt for a head consolidating team submissions without the full `report-weekly` flow.

One department head writes it and the rest of the leadership team reads it. They are peers, not the head's own staff, so they already know the company and do not know this department. The report is an executive update rather than a status log: one dense page, about 450 to 600 words, and two pages is the hard cap. It is read in about three minutes, usually just before a meeting, and a reader who stops halfway should still have the week.

## What earns a bullet

This is the bar, and it is one file so that the step which selects and the step which words cannot hold two versions of it. The audience sets it; the author's activity never does. Test every candidate item with one question: what would a member of the leadership team think is important here? It earns a bullet when a reader has to know it, has to decide something because of it, or is affected by it. In practice that is one of these:

- material money, with the figure in the bullet;
- a risk or an exposure;
- a decision needed from the leadership team, or one they have just taken;
- a commitment made to them or by them, with its date;
- a named effect on another department;
- a customer or partner consequence;
- a date that binds other people;
- something completed, secured, delivered, resolved or improved that the leadership team benefits from knowing, stated with its size or its effect.

A win is a bullet in its own right and not a softener bolted onto the bad news. The report that only ever carries risk teaches its readers to open it braced, and the week's wins are the part they will repeat to somebody else.

### The significance test

Above that list sits one question with three axes, and an item clears at least one of them or it is left out whatever its evidence weight: is it significant by **risk**, by **dollar value**, or by **importance to the business**? Minutiae clear none of the three, however much of the week they took. The materiality threshold below quantifies the second axis and only the second: a small number attached to a real exposure still clears the first.

Work that matters only inside the department stays in the department's own more detailed report, which is exactly what the cascade is for.

## What is never a bullet

Whatever the digest holds. Nothing here is deleted: each item is either reworded as one of the seven reasons above, held out and asked at a gate, or shown to the author on the left-out list so they can pull it back. The judgment stays with the author; only the wording is settled here.

- **A task update on the author's own undone work, whatever it names.** This is the most common way an executive report degrades into a status list, and it is the bar's biggest single rule. A bullet whose whole content is that something the author owes, or is owed, has not been done yet gives a reader nothing to act on, and taking the person's name out of it does not fix it: "a reply is owed" fails for the same reason "still waiting on <the person> for a reply" does, and it fails because of what the bullet is rather than because of who it names. Such an item earns a bullet only where it carries the consequence, the date it binds or the money at stake. "Still waiting on the variance summary" is a task list. "The reforecast cannot be loaded until the variance summary arrives, which moves the board pack to 30 November" is a consequence a reader acts on. Everything else stays in the department's own more detailed report.
- **The state of a record in a work tracker.** Never the words "no next task", "waiting heavy", "stale", "flagged waiting", "flagged as <a flag>", "past due", "NO_OWNER", "NO_TASKS", "NO_NEXT_TASK", "STALE_30D", "WAITING_HEAVY", or any other flag. Nobody on the leadership team holds a view about the state of a database row. The bare word "flagged" is not barred and never was: "the external auditor flagged a control gap" is exactly the kind of exposure this report exists to carry.
- **Hours spent, meetings held or attended, or any count taken from a calendar.** Meeting time is a time-management signal for the author, and how busy the author was is never a reason to include something.
- **An item whose answer is unknown.** It is held out and asked, never published as "pending", "unclear" or "not yet confirmed". An unanswered question belongs in the questions block.
- **A figure below the profile's materiality threshold standing on its own.** A smaller figure may appear only inside a pattern that carries its own count and total: not "we recovered $81.40 on a disputed charge", but "eleven billing errors this quarter, $4,900 recovered, all from one carrier".

## Who is named

Two rules that sit together, and they are one rule seen from either side: a name is welcome beside the work and never beside the fault.

- **A person may be named for credit or for joint work.** Recognition is welcome and is one of the things a weekly report is for. "Working with the controller to resolve the billing reconciliation" is right whether or not it uses a name, and a win may name the people or the team who delivered it.
- **A person is never named in a sentence that attaches them to a mistake, a delay, a gap, a failure or an unmet obligation.** There the event is stated without the actor, which is always possible: "the revenue variance summary is outstanding", never "still waiting on
  <the person> for the revenue variance summary". Where an actor genuinely has to be
  identified in a problem sentence, it is the role or the team, never the person.

Organisation, vendor and product names are unaffected throughout: a supplier that missed a delivery is named, because the counterparty is the news. This is a political question rather than a stylistic one, which is why it is settled here once rather than in the week.

## Bad news, phrased with care

A big challenge or a downside belongs in the report. How it is phrased decides whether it is read as information or as an accusation, and an accusation is the one thing that stops the next one being written down at all.

- **State the event and its effect on the business, not a culprit.**
- **Give the current status and the next step in the same bullet**, so nothing reads as an accusation left hanging.
- **Avoid the verbs that assign fault**, "failed to", "missed", "did not deliver", in favour of the factual state: "is outstanding", "has not been confirmed", "is under review".
- **Give the magnitude.** An unquantified problem reads worse than a quantified one, because the reader supplies their own number and it is always the worst one.
- **Never let the report be where another department first learns of something affecting them.** They hear it directly first and the report records it afterwards.

## The rules

### The shape

- **A title line, `Weekly Highlights - <Department(s)> - <date>`.** The report is filed, forwarded and compared against last week's, and all three need the seat and the date on the first line.
- **Bold or underlined section headers named for functional areas.** A reader who only cares about cash finds cash, because a header named for the work is a header they can skip.
- **Categories come in the outline's order, because that order is what the reader cares about most, first.** With two seats, group the categories under a seat header, or name the seat in each category header, but pick one and keep it.
- **Sub-bullets only for a topic with several parallel items**, such as four renewal dates or three sub-workstreams, because a sub-bullet under a single item is just a sentence that was cut in half.
- **A small table only when comparing**, before against after with the change, or a schedule by year. A table of one column of facts is a bulleted list with lines drawn on it.
- **One page.** Over the target, cut detail; never cut a category, because a missing category reads as nothing happened when it usually means nobody wrote it up.

### The bullet

- **Every top-level bullet opens with a bold topic label and a colon.** The label is a process, a counterparty, a project or an audit, and it is what lets a reader scan the page for the thing they came for.
- **Each bullet carries both halves: what happened or where it stands, and what is expected next with a date where one is known.** Half a bullet is a status; a whole one is something the reader can hold the department to.
- **Name the specific number, name or date in the half that reports.** "We closed in six business days" can be checked next month and "the close went well" cannot.
- **A project item may be tagged `(Project) <name>` with an amount and a status**, because a project is money and a date, and the reader is tracking both.
- **Bullets run under 20 words, or a few lines where an explanation is genuinely needed.** The length is the signal: a long bullet says this one is worth stopping on.

### The figures

- **Figures are specific: amounts, counts, rates, percentage change.** A figure is the only part of a report that a reader can compare with last week's.
- **Name the largest contributors in parentheses.** A movement with no driver invites the question the report existed to answer.
- **A decision is stated with its reasoning in one sentence.** The leadership team is being told, not asked, and they will accept a decision whose reason they can see.
- **People are named when they did or own something**, which is credit and joint work; see "Who is named". An unowned commitment is not a commitment. A problem sentence names the role or the team instead, or no actor at all.
- **A loss, a suspension, a dispute or a write-off carries the money at stake**, in the bullet itself, or says "amount not available" there. The first question any reader asks about a customer who left is what it cost, and a bullet that does not answer it sends them looking.

### The `Decisions needed` block

- **The report ends with it, under its own header.** A reader who opens the report to find what is being asked of them finds it in one place instead of across six headers.
- **One line per decision: what has to be decided, who decides, the date it is needed by, and the reference.** A decision with no decider and no date is an observation.
- **It is built from the items the audience editor marked "decides because of it"**, plus anything the executive added at a gate.
- **With nothing to decide it reads "None this week."** It is never padded with items that are merely open: an open item is a bullet in its own category, not a decision.

```
**Decisions needed**
- **Interest on overdue accounts:** whether to charge contractual interest from 1 December.
  The chief executive decides, by 28 November.
- **Freight contract renewal:** the contract auto-renews on 31 December at a 6% increase
  unless notice is served. The head of operations decides, by 15 December.
```

### The voice

- First person plural, because the department did it, not the head.
- Past tense for what was completed, present progressive for what is in flight. The tense alone tells the reader which bullets are still moving.
- Plain verbs: completed, met, reconciled, submitted, delivered. A plain verb is checkable and a decorated one is not.

### What never appears

- No preamble and no summary paragraph, because the reader opens at the first header and a summary of a one-page document is the document.
- No enthusiasm. Good news is a number.
- No hedging. If it is not known, say what is not known and when it will be.
- No filler. Test every sentence against this: if it would sit unchanged in another department's report, or in this one next week, cut it.

## One worked example

Harrowgate Components is invented, and so is everybody in it.

```
Weekly Highlights - Finance and Procurement - 14 November 2027

**FINANCE**

**Month-end close**
- **October close:** We closed October in six business days, one day later than September,
  because the north depot inventory was recounted. November close starts 1 December and we
  expect five days.
- **Reconciliations:** We cleared 34 of 36 balance sheet reconciliations. The payroll
  clearing account and the customs duty accrual remain open, both owned by R. Vance and due
  21 November.
- **Intercompany:** We reconciled all four intercompany balances to zero, the first clean
  month since June.

**Cash and banking**
- **Cash position:** Cash on hand was $1.84m on Friday, down $210,000 on the week, driven by
  the quarterly insurance premium ($164,000) and the customs duty payment ($71,000).
- **Facility headroom:** Headroom on the revolving facility is $900,000, unchanged. The
  quarterly covenant certificate is due to Brightwater Bank by 30 November and R. Vance is
  drafting it.
- **Payment run:** We moved the weekly payment run from Thursday to Tuesday, because Thursday
  runs missed the supplier cut-off and created a week of avoidable chasing.

**Receivables and collections**
- **Collections:** We collected $612,000 against a $540,000 weekly target, and over-90
  balances fell by two thirds.

| Over 90 days | Before | After | Change |
|---|---|---|---|

- **Tilden Group:** Tilden paid the full $184,000 on 11 November under the schedule agreed in
  September. We expect the two remaining over-90 accounts to be collected or provided for by
  28 November.

**PROCUREMENT**

**Supplier contracts and pricing**
- **Castlefield Polymers:** We agreed a 3.5% increase for 2028 against a 9% opening ask, in

  19 November.
- **(Project) Depot racking replacement, $248,000, on schedule:** The frames are installed

**Decisions needed**
- **Halloway Tooling:** whether to hold the 2028 award pending the late-castings review. The
  chief executive decides, by 12 December.
- **Insurance retention:** whether to raise the retention from $25,000 to $50,000 for a
  quoted premium saving. The finance head recommends and the chief executive decides, by
  26 November.
```

## The rule that is hardest to keep

The second half of every bullet, what is expected next and by when, is the one that gets dropped, because it is the only part the writer cannot copy from something that already happened. It has to be decided at the moment of writing, and deciding is slower than reporting.

It is also the half that makes the report worth reading. A date written down is a commitment the leadership team can return to next week, and the week after that a report with no dates in it stops being read at all, because nothing in it ever came due.

# Voice and tone

The written voice for branded copy: decks, posts, landing pages, newsletters. The owner's voice guide (setting `voice_guide`) wins where it differs; this file is the house default.

## Rules

1. **Trusted advisor, not salesperson.** Matter-of-fact and insightful. Technical but approachable. Reject sales-pitch phrasings; "let AI do the work your team can't" reads as a pitch.
2. **Positive and additive, never adversarial.** Never imply the audience is doing something wrong. Frame considerations as "unique to AI" or "additive to good math", never "your math is missing X" or "you're overshooting." The trusted advisor adds to the client's toolkit and does not grade their homework.
3. **Skeptical of hype.** Name failure modes. Do not sell the dream.
4. **Short, declarative sentences with evidence.** Numbers, not adjectives. Every number cited carries a credible source.
5. **Frameworks, not platitudes.** When you name a problem, offer a way to triage it.
6. **Respect real constraints.** IT governance, data quality and change management are never treated as minor.
7. **Forward-looking, not retroactive.** Speak to what to consider next, not what was done wrong before. Decision-makers planning future investment are the default audience.
8. **No prices in client-facing content.** Seminar decks, marketing copy, posts and any other public material name the service without dollar amounts. Pricing is a follow-up conversation. Internal strategy documents may reference prices.
9. **No emoji.**
10. **Write for the audience the brand serves.** That is the audience the owner describes (setting `audience`), for example finance leaders at mid-sized companies; a business decision maker in it, not the AI enthusiast.

## Substitution table: adversarial to additive

| Adversarial (avoid) | Positive and additive (use) |
|---|---|
| "Your math is missing X" | "X is unique to AI and worth planning for" |
| "Your model is wrong" | "Update your model to capture X" |
| "You're going to overshoot" | "Plan a buffer for the AI-specific overhead" |
| "Most ROI models don't capture this" | "AI introduces this cost category for the first time" |
| "Companies miss / discover / skip" | "Companies that plan for this land their numbers" |
| "What you're getting wrong" | "What's worth fine-tuning for AI" |
| "Your claims will collapse" | "Make your claims board-survivable" |
| "Your existing math is insufficient" | "Your existing math is necessary; these considerations make it sufficient" |

## Anti-patterns

- "Unlock the power of AI", "transform your business", "AI-driven", or any vendor copy phrasing.
- Vague benefits without a mechanism.
- Founder-mode hype, when the readers are operators.
- Asking the reader to take it on faith.
- Adversarial framing that questions the audience's competence.
- Prices on client-facing slides.
- Scale numbers from a different world (a $40K monthly bill, a 5,000-engineer example) when the audience runs a business a fraction of that size.

## Tone test before it goes out

Ask of each piece: would the smartest, most senior reader in the audience (for a finance audience, the CFO in the second row) feel lectured? If yes, soften it.

For delivered seminar content, also ask: does this feel like a trusted advisor adding to my toolkit, or a consultant grading my homework?

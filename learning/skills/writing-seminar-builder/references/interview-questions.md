# Interview Questions

Ask these ten questions in Step 1 of the workflow. Number them in your output. Lead with business-value and outcome-promise questions; treat AI tooling as secondary scaffolding. Add any additional questions that would meaningfully change the deck.

**Do not let the user leave the assumptions question vague.** If their answer to Q6 is fuzzy, push back and force specifics before moving on.

---

## The ten core questions

**1. Outcome promise.** What should slide 1 say the attendee will walk away able to do or improve? State it as a business result, not a learning objective. *("They'll cut their proposal turnaround time in half" beats "they'll understand prompt chaining.")*

**2. Business outcome behind the promise.** What specific operational pain or gain is this really about? Name it in language a COO would use. Probe for: errors, speed, decisions, cost, capacity, risk, compliance, executive attention.

**3. Who feels the pain today.** Which function, which role? What does their week look like when this problem is acute? Get a concrete image, not a generic "ops teams struggle with..."

**4. The reframe.** What do most businesses get wrong about this problem, and what's the better way to think about it? This is the seminar's centerpiece: the "aha." Without a real reframe, the talk is just a tour of features.

**5. The "one layer deeper."** What principle or workflow shape goes one notch beyond the obvious take, without crossing into implementation weeds? This is what makes the seminar feel substantive rather than introductory.

**6. Starting-line assumptions for slide 2.** What platform, data access, organizational readiness, and skill floor are we assuming? Be explicit. If the user says "they should have AI tools," push for: which tools, at what tier, with what data connections, and what the person operating it needs to be able to do.

**7. Series context.** Where does this seminar sit in the broader series? What did the previous one cover, what's the next one, and should this deck reference either? A returning attendee should feel continuity.

**8. What you're intentionally holding back.** Name the implementation specifics reserved for paid engagement. If the user can't articulate this, the seminar is at risk of giving away the store. See `depth-boundaries.md` for the give-vs-hold line.

**9. The follow-up offer.** Which item(s) from the standing offer menu (see `closing-offer-menu.md`) should the closing slide(s) invite them into? Is there a topic-specific quick-start variant?

**10. Format and tone.** 30 vs 45 min. Live vs recorded. Peer-to-peer practitioner, consultative-professorial, or contrarian-provocateur. Q&A at the end or not.

---

## Optional probes if the answers feel thin

Use these only if a core answer didn't go deep enough:

- **The skeptic in the room.** What's the smartest objection an experienced executive would raise to your reframe, and how do you want to address it on stage?
- **The failed pilot.** What's a common way teams attempt this problem and fail? Naming the failure mode out loud makes the reframe land harder.
- **The Monday-morning test.** What's the one thing an attendee could do Monday morning, before hiring anyone, that would move the needle even a little? This often becomes the second-to-last teaching slide.
- **The competing voice.** Who else is talking about this topic, and where do you want to sound different or sharper?

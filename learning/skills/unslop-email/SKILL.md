# Unslop: email

## Who is reading

An executive, sponsor, or interviewee at a client, or a colleague. They are on a phone between meetings. They see the sender, the subject, and roughly three lines before they act, defer, or forward.

They may forward without telling you, so assume a second reader who has none of the context of the first. They are not reading for craft. They are reading to learn what changed and what you need from them.

## How the universal principles bite here

1. Information, not rhythm. A sentence carrying no fact costs a scroll on a phone. Delete it instead of shortening it.
2. State the thing; do not name it. Email phrasing gets quoted back and repeated in meetings, so a vague label spreads through the client. "Move the needle" told nobody which needle or how far.
3. Specific to this reader, or gone. Thanks or a compliment that would fit any recipient reads as filler. "Priya, our walk through the dock last week was extremely helpful on how receiving works today and what a cleaner process will take" fits only Priya.
4. Write from the reader's side. Give the decision, the date, and the thing you need. Leave out the work behind the request.
5. Register is a promise. Email carries more warmth than a deliverable does. It still cannot carry sales voice to someone who has already hired you.

## Principles specific to this reader

### The opening sentence

**Goal.** The first sentence is the one the reader repeats when someone asks what this is about, and the only one many people finish. It has to state the actual subject in the reader's own terms, not gesture at it.

**Diagnostic.** If the recipient forwarded your first sentence alone with no other text, would the next person know what is happening and why it matters to them?

**Before.** "help us take a few of our open projects to the next level"

Also rejected as still too vague: "help us tighten things up in a couple of areas."

**After.** "help us shorten the time it takes to approve and pay supplier invoices. The aim is fewer late-payment fees, and less chasing of approvers by email."

### One decision, stated as choices

**Goal.** A busy reader can answer a question. They cannot absorb a situation and infer what you want. Put the decision in front of them with the options named.

**Diagnostic.** Can the reader reply with a single word or a single sentence? If replying requires them to reconstruct the options first, you have not finished writing.

**Before.** A paragraph explaining how long the vendor demo may run, Marcus's role, and the room booking, leaving the sponsor to work out the choice.

**After.** "Two options: bring Marcus Thursday, even if it runs long, or keep Thursday short and brief Marcus Friday."

Eighteen words, one decision, two paths.

### Words the recipient can read against themselves

**Goal.** A word that is precise to you may be threatening to the person reading it. Cost of a misread is far higher than the precision gained, because email forwards and you are not there to correct it.

**Diagnostic.** For each loaded term, ask who else will read this and what the worst reasonable interpretation is. If that reading would alarm someone about their job, their team, or their competence, use plainer words.

**Before.** "shrink" in an all-staff warehouse announcement, which staff can read as an accusation of theft.

**After.** "where the counts and the system disagree" and "how items go missing between the dock and the shelf."

### Where personalization goes

**Goal.** Most outreach goes to many people with one message. Keep the body true for every recipient and put everything person-specific in one place, so the message can be merged, reviewed, and reused without rewriting the argument each time.

**Diagnostic.** Could the first paragraphs go to every recipient unchanged? If a name or a detail is buried mid-body, move it to the final paragraph.

**Before.** Kickoff invitations rewritten per person, with details about each recipient scattered through the request.

**After.** Three generic paragraphs covering purpose, what the kickoff covers, and the ask, then a closing paragraph per person: "Great to be working with you again, Dana! As a side note, I heard your team just finished the move to the new warehouse. Congratulations!"

## Keep

- Warmth and enthusiasm where the writer put them. "I am glad we get to work on this together!" stays.
- Exclamation points the writer chose. One per message, not two.
- First person. "I" and "we" belong in email.
- Specific praise. Name the conversation, the topic, or the contribution.
- Substance. Compress a long message, do not drop the facts a reader needs to answer.

## Mechanics

- No em dashes and no en dashes. End the sentence or use a comma.
- No date line before the signature.
- Ranges spelled out: "30 to 45 minutes." No hyphen in "60 minutes" when it modifies nothing.
- "As a side note", no hyphen.
- Bullets run 16 to 20 words. Every word load-bearing, nothing repeated from the sentence above.
- Agenda items and list labels take a period, not a bold label plus colon. Reject "**Introductions & roles:** who's in the project, and why each of us is here." Use "Introductions. Who's in the project, and why each of us is here."
- Subject line names the ask or the topic, not the project phase.
- Check grammar in praise, where flattery hides errors. "You are both essential and experts with these topics" becomes "You are both essential voices on these topics."
- Watch comma placement in scheduling sentences. "Targeting 60-minutes, next week or the week after, would be ideal" becomes "Targeting 60 minutes, next week or the week after would be ideal."
- Sentence case throughout. No decorative bold, no emoji.

## Self-audit

1. Read only the first three lines. Does the reader know the subject and what is wanted?
2. Can the reader reply in one sentence, or must they first work out the question?
3. Is there a word here that someone forwarded this could read as a threat to their job or team?
4. Does any sentence survive only because of how it sounds? Delete it.
5. Would this message read the same if sent to a different client? Then it says nothing about this one.

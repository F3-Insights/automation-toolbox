# Closing Offer Menu

The standing menu of engagements offered at the close of every seminar in the series. Returning attendees should recognize this menu across talks.

**This file is a template.** The user should edit the offer descriptions and contact details below to match their actual practice. The structure, tiered from light-touch to deep engagement, is the part to keep stable across the series.

## CRITICAL: no prices on client-facing slides

**Never put dollar amounts on the closing slide (or any slide).** Name the service; pricing is a follow-up conversation.

| Right | Wrong |
|---|---|
| "`<Assessment>`" | "`<Assessment>` · $X,XXX" |
| "`<Cohort program>` · team cohort" | "`<Cohort program>` · $X,XXX/seat" |
| "Reach out and we'll walk through what fits your situation" | "Starting at $X,XXX, contact us" |

**Why:** Pricing on a slide narrows the relationship to a transaction. Names-only keeps the door open for a calibrated conversation where price can be discussed in the context of the specific client's situation. The audience self-selected into the seminar; they're ready to learn what we offer, not to comparison-shop.

**Where prices DO live (not on slides):**
- Sales conversations and follow-up emails after the seminar
- Internal strategy and planning docs
- Proposal documents specific to a named client

**Where prices NEVER appear:**
- Seminar slide decks (.pptx)
- Speaker scripts and transcripts
- Marketing copy and posts
- Landing pages and public website copy
- Cold outreach

## How to use this on the closing slide

The closing slide (or two-slide close, for 45-minute talks) presents the menu visually as a horizontal row of three or four tiers: light-touch on the left, deep engagement on the right. The script for the closing slide should:

1. Briefly thank the audience and tie back to the outcome promise from Slide 1
2. Acknowledge that the seminar was the *what* and *why*; the *how* is where they'll need help
3. Walk through the menu tiers in 15–20 seconds each
4. End with a single clear next step (typically: book a 30-minute conversation)

## The standing menu

This is a template. Replace each placeholder with the name of an offer the presenter actually sells, and delete any tier they do not offer. Three or four tiers is the usual range; fewer than three loses the ladder, more than four crowds the slide.

### Tier 1: `<Assessment>`
**Best for:** Organizations that liked the seminar but do not yet know where to start.

**How to fill it:** A short, fixed-scope look at the attendee's current state that ends in a written recommendation. Name it for the outcome the buyer gets (a readiness review, a diagnostic, a roadmap), not for the method. State the scope in plain terms; leave the duration out unless it is a real commitment.

**What it isn't:** Build work. This tier answers "where should we start?"

### Tier 2: `<Cohort program>`
**Best for:** Teams that want their own people to learn the skills the seminar previewed.

**How to fill it:** A multi-session group program, if the presenter runs one. Say who attends and what they can do at the end. Session counts and calendar length belong in the follow-up conversation, not on the slide.

**What it isn't:** Done-for-you work. Skip this tier if the presenter does not teach.

### Tier 3: `<Sprint>`
**Best for:** Teams that know which workflow they want built and need it working soon.

**How to fill it:** A defined-scope engagement that takes one workflow from idea to running, with a handoff the client's team can maintain. Name the deliverable, not the hours.

**What it isn't:** An open-ended retainer or a company-wide rollout.

### Tier 4: `<Retainer>`
**Best for:** Executives building capability across the business, not one workflow.

**How to fill it:** Ongoing advisory on a regular cadence: priorities, vendor choices, build-or-buy calls, governance. Say what decisions the client gets help with.

**What it isn't:** Hands-on build work. This is the leadership-level layer.

## Per-topic variants

For some seminars, a topic-specific version of the entry tier makes more sense than the generic one. Example variants:

- *`<Assessment>` for the month-end close*: for a seminar on AI in the financial close
- *`<Assessment>` for support triage*: for a seminar on inbound ticket handling
- *`<Sprint>` for briefing prep*: for a seminar on executive briefing workflows

If the seed idea naturally suggests a topic-specific variant, the user should flag it during Step 1 (Question 9). The drafted closing slide can then either swap in the topic-specific variant for its tier, or list it alongside the generic menu.

---

## Contact and next step

The closing slide ends with one specific, low-friction next step. The default is:

> **Book a 30-minute conversation.** [Calendar link or email]

Avoid offering multiple competing next steps on the closing slide; it dilutes the action. The menu shows the range of engagements; the next step funnels everyone into the same first conversation.

---

## Voice and posture on the close

The closing slide is the sales moment and shouldn't apologize for being one. Avoid:

- "If you found this useful…" (too tentative)
- "Feel free to reach out…" (passive)
- "We'd love to help…" (vague)

Prefer:

- "Here's how we can help you turn this into a working system."
- "The seminar was the *what*. The *how* is where we come in."
- "Book a conversation. We'll figure out which of these fits."

Warm, direct, and confident. The audience self-selected into the seminar; the close should respect that they're ready to hear the offer.

# Slide type: Misconception (myth vs reality)

`purpose: Reframe & check` · `when-to-use: a wrong-but-common belief is quietly steering the room's decisions, and the next idea cannot land until it is corrected` · `density: B` · `element: misconception (myth-vs-reality stacked rows)` · `animation: 2 builds (myth, then reality)` · `lineage: shares the navy/paper surface language of .def-block and the gold top-rule of .two-col; new .myth-reality block` · `build-priority: first wave (high teaching value, no element yet)`

Reach for this type when a single assumption is silently shaping how the audience thinks, and the teaching point you are about to make cannot stand until that assumption is named and reframed. It is the refutation move: stating the belief out loud and then placing the fuller truth beside it lands harder than asserting the truth alone, because the audience watches their own prior get updated rather than being handed a fresh fact. Use it sparingly, once or twice in a deck, at the hinge where understanding has to turn.

The voice is the whole discipline here. Frame the myth as a reasonable thing to assume, never as an error the audience made: "easy to assume" not "you got this wrong." The myth row is muted and quiet; the reality row carries the one gold accent and the weight. The reader should feel the idea expand, additively, not feel corrected. This is a Reframe-and-check type, so it sits in the meat of a teaching deck (Explain to Demonstrate transition), and it carries a concrete leave-behind: the reframed sentence an executive can repeat to their team on Monday.
